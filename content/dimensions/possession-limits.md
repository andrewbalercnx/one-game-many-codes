Every code has to answer one question before anything else about it makes sense:
once you have the ball, how long is it yours? The answers split the family in two —
codes that never count possession, and codes that put a number on it.

## Why it was introduced

Left unlimited, possession rewards holding on. In rugby league before 1966 that produced
strangulation football: a side could keep the ball almost indefinitely, and did. The
limited-tackle rule put a number on possession for the first time — and a number changes
how a thing is spent.

## On the pitch

Six tackles made possession scarce. Scarce things get spent carefully — that is why
league teams kick on the last. The gridiron codes reached the same idea by a different
route: a fixed count of downs to make the distance turned each possession into a
sequence of planned, discrete plays, with Canadian football's three downs forcing the
issue a down sooner than the American four. Where the count is absent — soccer, union,
Australian rules — possession is kept until it is lost, and the game flows without a
built-in reset.

## In the stands

A number you can see becomes a countdown. As the last tackle nears, everyone watching
knows the ball is about to change hands, and the tension climbs toward that moment —
a suspense the uncounted codes never manufacture in quite the same way.

## Adoption and rejection

Soccer never counted possession and shows no sign of starting. Union kept the contest
open at the breakdown rather than capping it. The gridiron codes made the count spatial —
yards to be gained — rather than a tally of tackles. One idea, a finite possession,
arrived at independently and expressed four different ways.
