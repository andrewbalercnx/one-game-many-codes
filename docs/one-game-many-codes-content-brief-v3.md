# One Game, Many Codes
## Content Brief v3.1 – content and outcomes

Title confirmed: **One Game, Many Codes** (previously working-titled *The Football Genome*). The genome metaphor survives as the internal and feature-level name for the Game DNA cards, where it earns its keep; the site's front-facing name now states the thesis plainly instead.

Companion documents: the navigation model and its adversarial review report. This revision folds the review's findings back into the content plan. One production fact is now fixed and shapes several sections: the narrated mode's talking head is video and audio recorded by the author.

---

# 1. Proposition

**Complex systems emerge from simple rules.**

That is the site's thesis, stated first because it is what distinguishes the project from every existing football history site. Football is not merely the subject matter – it is the most accessible demonstration of emergence available. The major codes evolved from closely related ancestors, the rule divergences are small and documented, and the resulting sports are radically different. History ran a controlled experiment; the site publishes the results.

The working question on every page: **how do rules create games?** The site's job is to make the causal chain visible – this rule, therefore this behaviour, therefore this tactic, therefore this body type, therefore this spectator experience.

The family tree is the navigation device. It is not the point.

---

# 2. The visitor journey

The organising principle of the whole site is the visitor's curiosity, not the content taxonomy. Every page is authored to answer exactly one question and to deliberately raise the next. A worked chain:

> Why does American football stop after every play?
> → Which rule caused that?
> → Why doesn't rugby do the same?
> → What would happen if rugby adopted it?

The last question is not rhetorical – wherever history has actually run the test, it resolves to a Natural Experiments page (§6, Layer 4). The Map is therefore a map of ideas rather than a taxonomy: nodes and edges are entry points into question-chains, and the intended movement through the site is lateral, following ideas across codes rather than drilling down within one.

**Prompts are authored, never templated.** Every substantial page ends with two to four onward questions, each a real link into another part of the site – and each written specifically for that page ("Why doesn't rugby just adopt downs?"), not generated from the recurring question-types. The adversarial review's finding stands: templated prompts become wallpaper within three pages, and the prompts are the site's actual navigation. The recurring types (which rule created this / has anyone removed it / who inherited it / what did this split from) survive as the author's checklist, invisible to the reader.

**Chains pay off as well as raise.** A journey that only ever owes the visitor the next answer fatigues. Each chain is authored with at least one designed payoff moment – a reveal that rewards the distance travelled. The exemplar payoffs are the shared-inheritance discoveries: the mark and the fair catch as two fossils of one ancestral rule, met just when the visitor has crossed from one code into the other. Payoffs are planned per chain at authoring time, not left to emerge.

**Deep entry is the majority case.** Most visits arrive at interior pages – from a search, a shared episode, a screenshotted compare. Every substantial page therefore orients a cold visitor in its first screen: which code and question it belongs to, one line of "this is part of a bigger story", and the door to the Map. A page that only works when reached from the homepage is misauthored.

This is a page-authoring discipline, not a navigation feature. A page that answers its question but raises nothing is unfinished. The behaviour it produces – readers crossing codes by following questions – is the site's primary success measure (§10).

## Two modes of travel

The site is consumed in two modes, and both are first-class:

- **Lean-forward** – conventional website navigation. Reading, exploring the Map, following prompts laterally across codes. The mode everything above describes.
- **Lean-back** – a narrated, podcast-style experience: the author on camera and voice, over rich imagery, simple on-screen text, and the same short annotated clips. An episode is a question-chain told aloud – the "answer one question, raise the next" structure is already an episode arc, and the end-of-page prompt becomes the cliffhanger that cues the next episode or drops the viewer into the browse site at exactly that question.

The narrated layer is curated, not exhaustive. Full parity – a narrated version of every page – would be expensive and mostly unwatched. Instead, a deliberate episode slate over the strongest material: the seven Natural Experiments (before/change/after is already a story arc), the flagship Consequence Chains, and a selected run of Rule Explorer dimensions framed as questions ("Why can't you throw the ball forwards?"). Reference-shaped content – the glossary, the timeline, the genome methodology – stays lean-forward only.

The modes feed each other, and that flow is measured (§10): episodes are the site's most shareable surface and its natural acquisition channel, and each one ends by opening a door into the browse experience; conversely, every episode's transcript and clip set live on the corresponding page, so the narrated layer adds no orphaned content.

# 3. What the site is not

- Not an encyclopaedia of football history. Wikipedia exists and is good at that.
- Not a rules reference. The laws of each game are documented by their governing bodies; the site links out rather than duplicating.
- Not an argument that any code is better. The neutrality applies to codes. It does not apply to claims about causation – the site takes positions on *why* things happened and defends them with evidence.
- Not a stats site. Numbers appear where they carry a causal argument, and nowhere else.

# 4. Canon and scope

## First-class codes (full treatment)

Every layer of the site covers these six:

1. Association football (soccer)
2. Rugby union
3. Rugby league
4. American football
5. Canadian football
6. Australian rules football

## Second-class codes (comparative treatment)

These get a code page and appear in the tree, Rule Explorer and DNA comparisons, but do not get exhaustive signature-action or timeline coverage:

- Gaelic football – included deliberately, not as a courtesy. It is the strongest evidence for convergent evolution in the family (a code that arrived at mark-like and solo-run mechanics from a partly separate line), and International Rules is a literal splice of two genomes – an annual experiment in hybridising rulesets.
- Rugby sevens – the cleanest natural experiment in the whole dataset: identical laws to union, one variable changed (players per side, 15 → 7), completely different sport. Sevens is almost a control group.
- Futsal – same relationship to soccer that sevens has to union: shrink the field, watch the technique change.

## Satellite codes (tree nodes only)

Arena football, flag football, touch and tag variants, walking football, beach codes, International Rules. Each gets a node in the tree with a two-paragraph "what changed and why" entry. No dedicated page at launch.

## Historical ancestors

Mob/medieval football, the public school games (Rugby, Eton, Harrow, Winchester), Cambridge Rules, Sheffield Rules. Treated as ancestors: covered for what they contributed and where they split, not as sports in their own right.

## Timeline scope

The timeline covers **rule changes and splits only** – the events that altered gameplay. Governing body formations appear where they caused divergence (the 1895 League split, the 1863 FA codification). World Cups, Olympics and competition history are out of scope. This cuts the timeline from an unbounded archive to roughly 80–120 dated events across all codes, each of which earns its place by having changed how the game is played.

# 5. Audience and reading depth

**The primary reader is the curious fan** – "I like rugby, why is American football so different?" – and every page must work for them first. The systems-thinking material is a layer on top, not a gate in front.

In practice each substantial page reads at two depths:

- **The surface read** – plain-language cause and effect, watchable in the clips, no prior knowledge of the other code assumed. A rugby fan can read the American football pages cold.
- **The deep read** – the emergence framing, the historiography, the data behind the DNA scores. Signposted, skippable, never required to follow the main argument.

Coaches, students and game designers are served by the deep read without needing pages of their own. The counterfactual prompt ("what would happen if this rule were removed?") is the one concession to the game-design audience, and it links to a real historical answer wherever one exists.

## The anchor vantage

The site is neutral between codes; the visitor doesn't have to be. On first meaningful interaction the site invites the visitor to name the football they know best – their **anchor code** – and frames the unfamiliar relative to it. This came out of the adversarial review's engagement pass and is the model's single strongest engagement device: it converts editorial neutrality into a personal vantage point, and it directly drives the cross-code traversal the site measures.

Anchoring is a content requirement, not only an interface one. It obliges:

- **Bridge lines** on every code page, one per anchor: a single sentence translating from each other code's vocabulary ("You know this as a knock-on; here it's a legal fumble"). Six first-class codes means five bridge lines per code page – short, authored, and among the highest-value sentences on the site.
- **Anchor-first glossary ordering**: an entry's shared-inheritance sibling from the visitor's anchor code surfaces first. This is also where the payoff moments (§2) most often land.
- Compare defaulting one side to the anchor.

# 6. Content architecture

Five layers, each with one job, plus three cross-cutting features. The test applied throughout: given a reader question, exactly one layer owns the answer.

**Two consequence lenses, never conflated.** Every causal claim on the site runs through one of two distinct lenses:

- **The gameplay lens** – what a rule does to play: tactics, flow, player roles, physique. Evidenced by match data and the historical record.
- **The fan lens** – what a rule does to the spectator: suspense, hope, the meaning of a result, the emotional texture of watching. Evidenced by a different literature entirely – sports economics and psychology (suspense/surprise modelling, loss aversion, the uncertainty-of-outcome debate) – with different methods and more contested findings.

The lenses are separated because they can disagree about the same rule. The three-points-for-a-win change failed on the gameplay lens (no measurable increase in goals or decisiveness) while still changing what a draw *means* to a fan. VAR barely alters gameplay while rewiring the sport's peak emotional moment. And the uncertainty-of-outcome literature shows the lenses can directly oppose: more competitive matches can mean *lower* home attendance, because what fans of a code value (upset possibility) is not what fans of a team turn up for (probable wins). Every claim knows which lens it sits in; no fan-lens claim is laundered through gameplay data or vice versa.

**The taxonomy is invisible.** This entire vocabulary – layers, lenses, tiers, confidence levels – is authoring machinery, and the adversarial review's simplicity pass established that none of it may surface as reader-facing language. The two lenses appear on pages as sections plainly titled **On the pitch** and **In the stands**. Evidence tiering appears as the natural register of the prose ("the record shows…" against "arguably…") plus a single small source marker. Internal names (Layer 4, tier 2, gameplay lens) never appear in the interface or the copy.

## Layer 1 – The Map

*Owns: descent and influence. Answers "where did this come from?"*

One artefact, two views:

- **Tree view** – strict descent, and the default. Medieval football → public school codes → modern codes, progressively disclosed. Every node expands. Every split is annotated with the specific rule disagreement that caused it (the 1863 hacking dispute, the 1895 broken-time payments schism, Camp's possession rules).
- **Graph view** – the honest version, reached by an explicit switch ("The messier truth"). Influence is not descent, and several codes have multiple ancestors. Australian rules draws on at least three contested sources. Dashed edges carry a confidence marker (§7) and link to the evidence. This is the expert view; first-time visitors meet the tree first.

Every node answers four questions: where did this come from, why did it split, what changed, what survived. The "what survived" question is underrated – the mark in Australian rules and the fair catch in American football are both fossils of the same ancestral free-catch rule, and spotting shared inheritance is half the fun of the site. Per §2, each node is an entry point into a question-chain, not a leaf in a hierarchy.

## Layer 2 – Code Pages

*Owns: each game as a whole. Answers "what kind of game is this and why?"*

One page per first- and second-class code. Structure per page:

- **The one-sentence genome** – the code's defining constraint, stated up front. Rugby league: possession is a finite resource, six chances long. American football: the game is a sequence of discrete planned plays. Soccer: hands are forbidden, so feet had to become hands.
- **Bridge lines** – one sentence per other first-class code, translating into that code's vocabulary (§5). Served according to the visitor's anchor.
- **How it got here** – descent summary linking into the Map.
- **The rules that matter** – not the full laws; the four to six rules that generate this code's character, each linking into the Rule Explorer.
- **Signature actions** – linking into the glossary.
- **The genome card** – the DNA scores, with the compare control.
- **Where it's still evolving** – recent and live rule change: union's 50:22 and tackle-height laws, the NFL's kickoff redesign, soccer's ongoing offside-technology arguments. This section matters twice over: the tree cannot read as finished history – the codes are still speciating – and this content is what the returning-visitor experience runs on (§10.4).

## Layer 3 – Rule Explorer

*Owns: cross-code comparison of a single rule dimension. Answers "how do the codes differ on X, and what follows?"*

The site's defining feature. Organise by rule, not by sport: each rule page takes one dimension and walks the reader across all codes. Rules are presented as causes whose effects ripple outward, never as regulations to be memorised.

**Every rule page follows the same template, with the two lenses held apart:**

1. The rule – what it says, in each code's version
2. Why it was introduced – the historical problem it solved
3. **On the pitch** – immediate effect on play, what compounded over decades, the behaviours it rewards, the behaviours it discourages
4. **In the stands** – what the rule does to the watching experience: the suspense profile it creates, whose hope it preserves or kills, how it changes the meaning of a score or a result. Sourced from the fan-lens literature, tiered under §7, and never inferred from the on-pitch section
5. Adoption and rejection – which other codes took it up, which refused, and why

The fixed template is what turns rules from static facts into engines of evolution, and it makes every rule page comparable with every other. Where the two lenses disagree about a rule, the page says so explicitly – those disagreements are among the most interesting content on the site.

Launch set of rule dimensions (twelve):

1. Forward passing – forbidden / free / restricted-per-down
2. Restart after tackle – play on / ruck contest / play-the-ball / dead-ball stop
3. Possession limits – unlimited / six tackles / four downs / three downs
4. Offside – strict line / moderate / minimal / dynamic
5. The free catch – mark / fair catch / none
6. Substitution – none-to-few / interchange / unlimited
7. Players per side – 7 / 11 / 13 / 15 / 18
8. Field dimensions and shape – including the Canadian 110-yard field and the AFL oval
9. Scoring structure – what scores, how much, and what the ratios reward
10. The tackle itself – what is a legal tackle, and what the tackler may do next
11. Kicking as a weapon – when kicking away possession is rational
12. Use of hands – the original 1863 fork

Game Flow is absorbed here as dimensions 2 and 3 seen from the spectator's side, plus one standing visualisation on every code page: ball-in-play share, possession changes per match, average passage length. Flow is an output of the restart and possession rules; it does not need its own section, it needs to be shown as their consequence.

## Layer 4 – Natural Experiments

*Owns: before/after evidence. Answers "how do we know rules cause this?"*

The layer that carries the site's evidentiary weight. The thesis is causal; static cross-code comparison can only ever show correlation. But the codes have repeatedly changed one rule and left the rest alone, and the before/after is documented. These are the strongest pages on the site, and the natural spine of the episode slate.

Launch set:

- **The 1925 offside change (soccer).** Three defenders to two. Goals in the Football League jumped by roughly a third in a single season, and Chapman's WM formation was invented as the defensive counter-move. One line in the law book; a formation revolution. **This is the canonical entry story**: the default first-visit question on the homepage, episode one of the slate, and the first page authored – it tells the whole thesis in one story, in the code with the largest audience.
- **The limited tackle rule (rugby league, 1966).** Unlimited possession had produced strangulation football. Four tackles (later six) made possession finite – and tactical kicking, the kick-chase and the entire modern shape of league follow from that scarcity.
- **The legal forward pass (American football, 1906).** Introduced to reduce deaths in the mass plays. The game's entire aerial dimension, the quarterback as the central figure, the passing route tree – all downstream of a safety reform.
- **The back-pass law (soccer, 1992).** Keepers could no longer handle back-passes. Time-wasting collapsed, keepers became footballers, and thirty years later the sweeper-keeper and playing-out-from-the-back are orthodoxy.
- **Three points for a win (soccer, England 1981).** The failure case, and deliberately so – the other experiments all show rules working, which quietly overstates the thesis. The rule was introduced explicitly to force attacking play. The evidence says it largely didn't: analyses across multiple leagues found no increase in mean goals, and prospect-theory work attributes the failure to loss aversion – managers' preference for avoiding defeat survived the changed reward. Written across both lenses: on the pitch the rule failed; in the stands it succeeded anyway, permanently changing what a draw means. The page's argument: rules propose, psychology disposes – constraint changes interact with human loss aversion rather than dictating behaviour, which is itself a claim about how emergence works.
- **Free substitution (American football, 1940s, permanent 1950).** The rule that created two-platoon football and, from it, the extreme positional specialisation the site's flagship consequence chain describes.
- **Sevens as a standing experiment.** Not a rule change but a permanent A/B test: union's laws, half the players, and a sport with different athletes, different tactics and a different audience.

Format per page: the state before, the change itself (dated, quoted where the law text is short), what happened next, and what it demonstrates about the site's thesis. Each page cites primary or strong secondary sources – these pages make the site's boldest claims and must be its best-sourced.

The counterfactual prompt on other pages resolves here whenever history has actually run the test.

## Layer 5 – Consequence Chains

*Owns: multi-step emergence stories. Answers "why is this whole phenomenon the way it is?"*

Long-form pages tracing a chain from rule to visible phenomenon. This is the intellectual heart of the site, and the chains deliberately extend beyond physique and specialisation into everything downstream of rules – coaching philosophy, injury profiles, broadcasting economics, cultural identity, fan psychology. Small changes propagate through an entire sporting ecosystem, and the chains are where that propagation is shown end to end. Each chain declares (internally) which lens it runs in, and each is authored with its payoff moment planned (§2).

**Launch set (five):**

- Why American football has specialists (free substitution → short maximal plays → mass → platoons)
- Why Australian footballers are endurance athletes (interchange limits + field size + continuous play)
- Why rugby forwards are heavy (scrum, ruck and maul → repeated collision → mass advantage)
- Why soccer selects for smaller players (running economy under continuous play + no handling)
- Why league and union diverged so far from a shared 1895 starting point (the ruck/play-the-ball fork compounding for 130 years – the best illustration on the whole site of small differences amplifying)

**Second wave (committed, post-launch):**

- **Why soccer fans can always hope.** A fan-lens chain, and the best-evidenced outside the launch set because the quantitative work already exists (Vicente et al. 2025, *Journal of Sports Analytics*; Ben-Naim et al. 2006; Ely, Frankel & Kamenica 2015 on suspense and surprise; Dolton & MacKerron on loss asymmetry). Rules that suppress scoring → few scoring events per match → each match is a small sample → variance dominates skill → underdogs win far more often. Measured across World Cup data, soccer's underdog achievement score is roughly 0.27 against rugby's roughly 0.04 – nearly an order of magnitude apart within the site's own canon. Downstream: cup-magic folklore, giant-killing culture, and a fan psychology of permanent hope, against rugby's fan psychology of anticipated quality. The page must carry the fork the uncertainty-of-outcome literature demands: what fans of a *code* value (upset possibility) diverges from what fans of a *team* attend for – Premier League evidence finds home fans prefer probable wins over close games. Stated confound, per §7: the rugby figure comes from World Cups where the tier-1/tier-2 talent gulf is structural; the scoring-frequency mechanism is the tier-2 claim, the folklore claims are tier 3.
- **Why the codes injure differently.** Tackle rules → collision profile → characteristic injury patterns. This chain is unusual because the causal arrow now runs both ways: union's tackle-height laws are a live rule change driven by injury data, so the page loops directly back into Natural Experiments. Rules made the injuries; injuries are now remaking the rules.
- **Why American football fits television.** Discrete plays → natural ad breaks → broadcast revenue → rules changed *for* television (the two-minute warning). A consequence chain that ends with the consequences feeding back into the rulebook.
- **Why the codes coach differently.** Planned discrete plays → playbook culture and scripted openings; continuous play → principles-based coaching and in-game player autonomy.
- **Why codes become national identities.** The most speculative chain, written under the tier-3 evidence standard (§7) with the interpretive claims flagged as such. Gaelic football's role in Irish cultural nationalism and Australian rules as a marker of Australian distinctiveness are the anchor cases.

The division of labour with Layer 3: a rule page covers one rule across many codes; a chain page covers one phenomenon across many rules. Chains cite rule pages as their links. No chain restates a rule page's content.

## Cross-cutting feature A – Game DNA and Compare

The genome card metaphor, with methodology declared. Axes split into two classes:

**Measured axes** – derived from match data, sourced, with the method published:
- Ball-in-play share (the spread is dramatic and carries the argument on its own: roughly 11 minutes of 60 in the NFL against 55-plus of 90 in elite soccer – exact figures to be sourced per competition and season, and dated, because they move)
- Scoring events per match
- Possession changes per match
- Players per side, substitution allowance, field area per player
- Outcome randomness – the probability of a weaker team beating a stronger one, using the underdog-achievement methodology published in the sports-analytics literature (Vicente et al. 2025). This axis is unusual in being both measured and psychologically loaded: it is the genome's proxy for "can fans of a weak team hope?", and it links directly to the fan-psychology consequence chain.

**Editorial axes** – judgment calls, labelled as such:
- Specialisation, territorial emphasis, set-piece reliance, kicking importance

Every genome card carries a one-line provenance note and links to the scoring method. The explicit split adds credibility and invites argument about the scores rather than suspicion about how they were produced.

**Compare** is the DNA feature's second mode and deserves prominence in the interface: any two codes side by side – genome cards, shared ancestor from the Map, the rule dimensions where they differ, flow visualisation overlaid – with every point of difference linking to the rule page that explains *why* the difference exists. Every comparison should invite another, and one side defaults to the visitor's anchor code. The compare view is the site's designed share unit: composed to be screenshotted with its attribution and URL intact, because it will be screenshotted either way.

A deliberate constraint, held against the temptation to expand this into a content type: Compare is a **view assembled from content that exists once**. Seven-plus codes give twenty-one-plus pairings; authored prose per pairing is a combinatorial trap, and the "why" behind every difference already has an owner in the Rule Explorer. The only per-pair prose Compare generates is the shared-ancestor summary, which comes from the Map.

## Cross-cutting feature B – Signature Actions glossary

One entry per action (mark, play-the-ball, scrum, lineout, ruck, maul, snap, blitz, waggle, solo, press, offside trap, kick-chase, and so on – roughly 40–50 at launch across the canon). Actions are referenced from everywhere, so they live as a glossary rather than a section.

Each entry answers the same question: **why does this exist in this code, and why doesn't it exist elsewhere?** Structure: definition, the rule that makes it exist, why it evolved, one clip, one common misconception, and – where it applies – the shared-inheritance note linking sibling actions across codes, surfaced anchor-first (§5). The mark / fair catch pairing is the exemplar entry: same ancestor rule, two fossils, two codes – and, met mid-journey, the site's model payoff moment. **No glossary entry is a dead end**: every entry closes with its door – the sibling action, or the rule page that makes it exist.

## Cross-cutting feature C – Timeline

Scoped per §4: rule changes and splits only, ~80–120 events, each entry linking to the rule page or natural-experiment page it belongs to. The timeline is an index into the site, not a parallel narrative.

# 7. Editorial policy

## Contested history

The family tree simplifies genuinely disputed historiography, and the site will be judged by how it handles the disputes. Policy:

- **Confidence markers on descent claims.** Edges in the Map are marked established / probable / contested. "Rugby league split from union in 1895" is established. "Australian rules drew on Gaelic football" is contested, and the edge says so and links to a short evenhanded note on the evidence. Confidence apparatus lives in the Map's graph view (§6, Layer 1) rather than confronting first-time visitors.
- **Named myths get named.** The Webb Ellis story is presented as what it is – a foundation myth constructed decades later – because handling it well is a credibility signal to exactly the readers the site most wants. The Marn Grook question is presented with the scholarship on both sides and no false resolution.
- **No invented tidiness.** Where the record is genuinely unclear, the site says "unclear" rather than choosing the neater story.

## Evidence standards

Three tiers, and every causal claim knows which tier it sits in:

1. **Documented** – dated law changes, quoted rule text, sourced match data. Natural Experiment pages live here.
2. **Strong inference** – consequence chains where the mechanism is clear and widely accepted (substitution → specialisation). Presented confidently, mechanism shown.
3. **Plausible reading** – the site's own interpretive claims, including the cultural-identity chain. Flagged with "arguably" or equivalent, never dressed as tier 1.

Fan-lens claims carry an additional sourcing discipline: they cite the psychology and sports-economics literature directly (suspense/surprise modelling, loss-aversion studies, the uncertainty-of-outcome debate), and where that literature is internally contested – as the uncertainty-of-outcome evidence genuinely is – the page says so rather than picking the convenient side. An on-pitch fact about a rule is never used as evidence for an in-the-stands claim about the same rule; each lens earns its claims from its own evidence base.

Tier names and lens names are internal (§6): in the published prose, tiering surfaces as register and sourcing, never as labels.

## Language: from the UK, to the world

The language strategy mirrors the game's own journey. The site is authored in British English because the codes' common trunk is British – the 1863 fork, Sheffield, Cambridge, Rugby School – and the Map radiates outward from that point. Localised editions follow the same outward direction, prioritised by where the codes actually live now: Spanish and Portuguese first (soccer's centre of gravity), French next (soccer and rugby both), others as the audience justifies. English-only at launch, with localisation a stated intention rather than an afterthought.

The stance that governs every word of it: **origin is not ownership.** The site must never read as "we gave this game to you folks" – and the historical record forbids that framing anyway:

- The rules left Britain; the games were made everywhere. The receiving cultures were co-authors, not recipients. Brazil remade soccer's aesthetic, America rebuilt rugby football into a different sport entirely, and Melbourne codified Australian rules in 1859 – four years before the FA existed. The gift narrative isn't only patronising; on the evidence, it's false.
- Innovation is credited where it happened, whichever direction it travelled. The WM formation is English; Total Football is Dutch; the forward pass is American; the games' most consequential rule changes since 1900 mostly happened away from the origin point.
- The tree's root is a dating fact, not a claim of ownership. The site's own thesis makes this natural: Britain exported rulesets, and local conditions – the constraints again – produced divergent evolution. The journey outward is a story of transformation, not transmission.
- Localised editions are not translations of a British site looking outward. Each edition leads from its own vantage – the anchor-code machinery (§5) does this structurally, so a Brazilian edition anchors soccer by default and meets the rest of the family from there.
- Signature actions keep their names in every language. A maul is a maul, a waggle is a waggle, jogo bonito is jogo bonito; the glossary translates the explanation, never the vocabulary.

# 8. Voice

- Every page leads with what changed, not what is.
- **Every substantial page must be tellable.** The read version and the episode script are the same causal narrative in two registers; if a page can't be narrated aloud as a story of cause and effect, it's structured as an encyclopaedia entry and needs rewriting. This is the cheapest test of whether a page obeys the "what changed?" rule.
- **Scripts are written for one voice – the author's.** The narrated mode is presenter-led, recorded by the author, so episode scripts are written to be spoken by him in his own register, not in a neutral broadcast voice. The prose and the scripts should sound like the same person because they are.
- Spoken register follows the same rules as prose: cause stated plainly, one idea per beat, jargon glossed the moment it's spoken with the term shown as simple on-screen text.
- Every page's first screen orients a cold arrival (§2, deep entry); every page ends by raising its next question, authored specifically (§2). A page with no onward prompt is unfinished.
- Cause stated plainly: "Six tackles made possession scarce. Scarce things get spent carefully – that is why league teams kick on the last."
- Numbers used sparingly and only when they carry an argument.
- The reader is assumed intelligent and assumed to know none of the other codes. Jargon from any code is glossary-linked on first use, always.
- British English throughout, including for the American and Canadian code pages – per the language policy (§7): the source register reflects the direction of the game's journey, and never its ownership.

# 9. Media policy

Video is essential – the mark, the play-the-ball and the scrum cannot be conveyed in prose – and it never stands alone.

- One clip per concept, 10–60 seconds, from official or licensed channels, chosen to show a single thing. No highlight reels.
- Every clip answers a question, and its annotation points at the rule responsible for what the viewer is seeing.
- **Every clip-bearing page must survive its clip dying.** Clips get region-blocked, taken down and re-licensed. Each embedded clip is paired with a static annotated diagram or frame-sequence that carries the same explanatory load. The clip is the best version of the explanation, never the only version.
- Quarterly link-rot review of the clip inventory as a standing content task.

**The narrated layer** (§2, "Two modes of travel") reuses this same media inventory rather than building a second one, and is presenter-led: the talking head is video and audio recorded by the author.

- Episode format: the author on camera and voice, rich imagery, simple on-screen text, and the one-concept clip library as the visual spine. The per-clip annotation requirement ("which rule made this happen") converts directly into narration script – the clip library and the episode scripts are two outputs of one authored asset.
- Episodes are short and single-question, matching the page discipline: one question answered, the next raised as the closing beat. No omnibus episodes.
- **Cadence: weekly**, stated on the episode hub and kept. The honest arithmetic: the launch slate of 12–15 episodes is one season of roughly a quarter, so the pipeline must not stop there – season two draws on the second-wave chains, further Rule Explorer questions, and the evolution updates (§10.4), and scripts are banked several weeks ahead of recording so a missed session never breaks the cadence. Weekly is the most demanding option on the table; the segment-based batch recording below is what makes it survivable.
- **Narration is recorded in question-sized segments**, not continuous takes. This is the presenter-led version of the clip-death rule: when a clip is pulled or a "still evolving" fact changes, the episode is re-edited around modular segments rather than re-recorded whole. Recording sessions batch multiple episodes' segments, because presenter time is the narrated layer's scarcest resource and its true rate-limiter.
- Every episode's transcript is published on (or as) its corresponding page, which keeps the two modes in sync, makes the narrated content searchable and quotable, and covers accessibility without separate effort.
- **Audio syndication**: an audio-only derivative of each episode feeds standard podcast platforms. On-site podcast-style viewing and actual podcast listening are different habits; both are cheap once the recording exists.

# 10. Outcomes

What success looks like, in order of importance:

**1. Cross-code traversal.** The site's premise is that understanding comes from crossing codes, and §2 makes that the authored experience. The primary behavioural measure: share of sessions that visit pages from two or more codes, and share that follow at least one end-of-page prompt. If readers arrive on the rugby pages and only ever read rugby, the site is functioning as an encyclopaedia and failing at its actual job.

**2. Credibility with the hardest audience.** Historians of sport and knowledgeable fans of each code will check the contested-history handling first. Outcome: the Map's confidence markers and the myth-handling hold up to expert scrutiny – measured bluntly by corrections received, and by whether the site earns citation and inbound links from people who know the history.

**3. The deep read finds its audience.** Natural Experiment and Consequence Chain pages – the intellectually distinctive content – earn disproportionate dwell time and are what gets shared. If the sharing is all genome-card screenshots, the descriptive layer is winning over the causal one and the balance needs attention.

**4. Return visits driven by evolution.** The "where it's still evolving" sections give the site a live edge: each significant real-world rule change (a new NFL kickoff format, a union law trial) is an occasion for a new or updated page, a candidate episode segment, and a reason to come back.

**5. Mode crossover.** The narrated layer succeeds if it feeds the browse experience, not if it merely accumulates views: measured by episode completion, and by the share of episode viewers who enter the site through the door each episode opens. If episodes are watched but never followed into the site, the narrated layer is functioning as a detached channel rather than a mode of the same site – acceptable as marketing, but short of the design intent.
