# The ball as the deepest rule — dimension 13 and episode 1

Design-surface content note (round 2c, per DESIGN.md DECISION 20). Evidence-tier discipline applies throughout; every number below is `verified: false` until the author's sourcing pass.

## The thesis

Before any code wrote its first law, the family had already forked on a manufacturing and specification choice: the shape of the ball. A sphere rolls true; a prolate spheroid bounces chaotically and sits in the arms. Every code's laws specify its ball — the shape is not custom, it is statute — which makes the ball a rule dimension like any other, and arguably the one all the others sit on. The asymmetry of the football world — one globally dominant code, a family of regionally rooted handling codes — begins, though it does not end, here.

## Dimension 13: The ball

Question: what shape is the ball — and what did that decide?

Positions (per-code, law-cited at data entry): sphere — association football, futsal, Gaelic football; prolate spheroid, blunter — rugby union, rugby league, rugby sevens, Australian rules; prolate spheroid, pointed — American football, Canadian football.

On the pitch: a true-rolling sphere makes ground passing, first-time striking and dribbling viable, so a no-hands game works; a chaotic bounce makes carrying the reliable option, so the handling codes radiated around possession-in-hand; the pointed American ball rewards the spiral throw — and its shape sharpened AFTER the 1906 forward-pass legalisation, a rules-reshape-equipment exhibit linking this dimension to the natural-experiments layer.

In the stands: the sphere's game needs one ball and any flat ground — the cheapest possible adoption — and adoption compounds: common rules travel, standardisation wins (FA to FIFA to World Cup), audience brings money brings audience. These are economics-and-history claims and are sourced as such, separately from the biomechanics.

## The honesty clause

The ball enables; it does not decree. Gaelic football is the in-canon control case — round ball, hands allowed, regionally rooted — so the sphere alone did not globalise soccer. The dominant code needed the sphere PLUS radical simplicity PLUS Britain's trade and imperial networks PLUS first-mover standardisation, compounding. Episode 1 states the confounds on air; "the ball did it" is never asserted flat. Biomechanics claims (kicked-ball speed versus sprint speed; kick versus throw power for a large ball; roll predictability) are measurable and cited; adoption-economics claims carry the interpretive register.

## Episode 1 — "Why did one football conquer the world?" — six beats

1. Cold open, the presenter's own vantage: a rugby fan's question. My football is played seriously by a dozen nations. Theirs is played by all of them. Why?
2. Two balls on the table. One rolls true, one will not roll at all. The deepest rule in the family was settled before the first law was written.
3. On the pitch — what the rolling ball allows: a kicked ball outruns any player; power and distance from the leg; the ground pass; a game that never needs hands.
4. What the non-rolling ball rewards — carrying — and what carrying radiated: union, league, sevens, the Australian game, the American and Canadian games. One ball shape, six codes' worth of innovation.
5. In the stands — the compounding: one ball, any ground, common laws; FA to FIFA to the World Cup; money follows audience follows simplicity. Then the honesty beat: the ball opened the door — empire, industry and standardisation walked through it. And the control case: Gaelic's round ball stayed regional. The ball enables; it does not decree.
6. Closing question — so once the ball was chosen, what did the laws do? One line in one law book, 1925, is the cleanest answer ever recorded → episode 2.
